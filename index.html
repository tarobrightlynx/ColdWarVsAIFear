This dashboard summarizes the paper’s comparison of Cold War nuclear fear, AI fear in 2025, and AI fear in the April–June 2026 article sample. The main pattern is clear: Cold War fear centers on survival and global destruction, while AI fear increasingly centers on work, information, surveillance, infrastructure, and loss of human control.

Key interpretation

The paper’s central finding is not that technological fear disappeared. Instead, fear changed form. Cold War fear asked whether technology would destroy civilization. AI fear asks whether technology will replace, monitor, mislead, govern, or make decisions for humans.

How to read the category scores

Category scores range from 0 to 1. A value of 0.899 means the category appeared at an average rate of about 89.9% across the coded documents and model runs. Because one document can contain multiple fears, category percentages do not need to add to 100%.

What changed from AI 2025 to AI June 2026?

The AI comparison suggests that fear declined in intensity, but it did not disappear. It became more normalized and more tied to concrete systems.

Overall fear decreased

Mean fear intensity dropped from 5.895 in AI 2025 to 3.972 in AI June 2026. This was statistically significant.

Loss-of-control fear dropped

Delegated agency / loss of human control fell from 0.790 to 0.397, suggesting less dramatic AI-takeover framing in the 2026 sample.

Practical concerns rose

Surveillance, information disorder, infrastructure anxiety, and “none” were higher in 2026, pointing to more specific and sometimes lower-fear coverage.

Method summary

The dashboard translates the paper’s method and results into a public-facing web layout.

Study design

  • Datasets Cold War sources covered nuclear weapons, fallout, civil defense, and geopolitical conflict. The 2025 AI dataset covered modern AI concerns. The 2026 sample included 51 recent AI articles collected from April to June through a BigQuery/GDELT-based workflow.
  • Coding process Each source was coded using an LLM-assisted content analysis prompt. The paper describes repeated assessments and source-level averages to reduce variation.
  • Fear framework The 10 categories were Survival, Existential Dread, Global Extinction, Economic Crisis, Geopolitical Instability, Surveillance, Delegated Agency/Loss of Human Control, Information Disorder, Infrastructure Anxiety, and None.

Limits to keep in mind

The scores should be read as a structured analysis of expressed fear in text, not as a perfect measurement of actual human emotion. Differences in document type, source collection method, model interpretation, and scraped article availability can affect the results.
